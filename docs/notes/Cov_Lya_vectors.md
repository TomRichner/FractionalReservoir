Covariant Lyapunov vectors (CLVs) are the directions in tangent space that each carry exactly one Lyapunov exponent and that the dynamics maps into themselves. If you perturb the state along the i-th CLV at time t, the linearised flow carries that perturbation onto the i-th CLV at any later time, and its length grows or shrinks at rate λ_i. They are the Oseledets splitting made concrete: a basis of the tangent space, one vector per exponent, defined intrinsically by the dynamics rather than by the algorithm you used.

**How they differ from the vectors QR gives you.** The orthonormal basis that Benettin or the QR method carries along is what the literature calls the Gram–Schmidt or backward Lyapunov vectors. Only its first column is a true dynamical direction, the leading CLV. The rest are artefacts of the orthonormalisation: the second vector is "whatever is left of the second direction after projecting out the first", and so on. They depend on the ordering, are orthogonal by construction rather than by dynamics, and are not carried into each other by the flow. CLVs are generally not orthogonal, and their angles are physically meaningful.

**How they are computed.** The standard algorithm is Ginelli et al. (2007), with Wolfe and Samelson (2007) as an alternative. Ginelli's method runs the usual forward QR pass and stores the R matrices, then does a backward pass in which a random upper-triangular matrix is pushed backwards through the stored R's; it converges onto the coefficients that express the CLVs in the forward Gram–Schmidt basis. So they cost the forward QR (which you would be doing anyway for a top-K spectrum) plus a backward iteration of comparable length, plus storage of the Q and R sequences. They are obtained for the top K just as the exponents are.

**What they are useful for.**

- **Localisation of instability.** Each CLV tells you *which state variables* carry a given exponent: which neurons, and whether the unstable direction lives in the dendritic states, the adaptation variables, or the depression variables. For this network that would say whether the chaos is spread across the population or concentrated in a few units, and whether adaptation directions participate in the expanding subspace at all.
- **Hyperbolicity and tangencies.** The angle between the expanding and contracting CLV subspaces measures how hyperbolic the system is. Frequent near-tangencies between stable and unstable directions are the signature of non-hyperbolic dynamics, which is exactly the situation where finite-time exponents fluctuate strongly. For the intermittent single-timescale regime, whose 10 s exponent scatters by ±0.2, the CLV angle distribution would say whether that scatter is tangency-driven.
- **Slow bundles and physical modes.** In multiscale systems the CLVs separate into a "physical" set that spans the attractor's inertial manifold and a set of strongly contracting, decoupled modes. The number of physical modes is a dimension estimate that does not depend on the Kaplan–Yorke interpolation, and for a network with 10 s adaptation timescales the split between fast dendritic modes and slow adaptation modes would be visible directly.
- **Predictability and error growth.** Since a CLV is the shape a perturbation actually takes as it grows, the leading CLV is the direction in which forecast error develops, which matters for reservoir-computing readouts and for interpreting the memory-capacity results.
- **Response to perturbations.** In non-equilibrium and climate-style applications they are used to predict which stimuli the system responds to most strongly, since a stimulus with a large projection on the leading CLVs is amplified.

**For this project** they are not needed for anything now on the list. The exponents, the entropy rate and the Kaplan–Yorke dimension all come from the Gram–Schmidt basis without the backward pass. That is why the plan marks them out of scope. The case for adding them later is the localisation question, whether the unstable directions live in x or in the adaptation states, and the tangency question in the intermittent regime. Both would be a natural extension once the top-K routine exists, since Ginelli's backward pass needs only the R matrices that routine already computes and could store.

User: Are they local?

Agent: Yes, and that is exactly where they earn their keep. Covariant Lyapunov vectors are defined at every point along the trajectory, not just asymptotically. Oseledets' theorem gives a splitting of the tangent space at each state x(t) into subspaces E₁(x), E₂(x), … and the CLVs are basis vectors of those subspaces at that instant. The asymptotic exponent λ_i is the long-time average of the local growth rate of the i-th CLV, but the vector itself, and its instantaneous growth rate, exist pointwise.

Three consequences for an intermittent regime like the single-timescale one:

**The vectors are local; the labels are global.** The CLV called "unstable" is unstable in the long-time average. During a quiet stretch it may be contracting, and during a burst it expands strongly. So at any instant you have the direction and its finite-time growth rate, and you can watch that rate alternate between the burst and quiet phases. This is precisely a local decomposition of the finite-time Lyapunov exponent you have already been measuring with Benettin, resolved by direction rather than lumped into one number.

**They are covariant, so the local picture is consistent in time.** Since the flow maps the i-th CLV at t onto the i-th CLV at t + s, the vector you follow through a burst is the same dynamical object you followed through the preceding quiet stretch. The Gram–Schmidt vectors from QR do not have this property: after a burst the orthonormalisation re-mixes them, so their instantaneous growth rates are not attributable to one direction across time.

**Tangencies are the local signature of intermittency.** In an intermittently chaotic system the expanding and contracting subspaces typically come close to each other at particular points along the trajectory. The angle between the leading CLV and the contracting bundle is a pointwise quantity, and near-tangencies are where the local exponent fluctuates most and where finite-time estimates scatter. So the CLV angle time series would tell you whether the ±0.2 scatter in the 10 s exponent of the single-timescale regime is tied to specific tangency events, and whether those coincide with the bursts.

Two caveats. First, computing them does require a full forward pass and a backward pass over a long stretch, so although the resulting vectors are local, you cannot get them from a short window alone; the backward iteration needs enough time to converge before the segment you care about. Second, the Oseledets splitting is only guaranteed almost everywhere with respect to the invariant measure, and strict intermittency between two regimes can make the splitting badly conditioned at the transitions, which shows up as near-tangencies rather than as a failure to define the vectors. In practice Ginelli's method handles that; the angles just get small.
